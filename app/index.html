Where the word "neuron" comes from

Your brain has about 86 billion nerve cells called neurons. Each one receives signals from other neurons through connections called synapses. Some synapses push the neuron toward firing (excitatory). Others push it away from firing (inhibitory). When enough excitation adds up, the neuron fires: it sends an electrical pulse down its axon to the next neurons.

In 1943, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts described a simplified, mathematical version of this. Every network in this lab is built from that simplified version. Try it: click A, B, or C to make that neuron fire, and change the synapse strengths and the threshold.

    Where the comparison stops working
    • Scale. A brain has about 86 billion neurons and roughly 100 trillion synapses. The network in Level 4 has 42 neurons.
    • Timing. Real neurons fire in pulses over time. An artificial neuron outputs one number per input.
    • Learning. The networks in this lab learn by backpropagation (Level 3). There's no evidence that brains learn this way, and how brains learn is still an open research question.
    • The name. "Neural network" makes AI sound more like a brain than it is. Keep that in mind when you read about AI.

      The learning rule

      For each training example: add a fraction (0.2) of the input to the correct neuron's weights. If the network's answer was wrong, also add the full input to the correct neuron's weights and subtract it from the weights of the neuron that wrongly won.

      This is a version of the perceptron learning rule, published by Frank Rosenblatt in 1958.

      Adding a hidden layer

      In Level 2, each output neuron had one picture to match the whole digit. People write 7s in many ways, and one picture can't cover them all. This network adds a hidden layer of 16 neurons between the input and the output. Each hidden neuron looks at all 196 pixels. Each output neuron looks only at the 16 hidden neurons.

      Each hidden neuron squeezes its weighted sum into a number between 0 and 1 with the sigmoid function. That number is its activation. The weights start random. Training uses backpropagation, the subject of part 3 of the 3Blue1Brown series.

      The sigmoid function

      Sigmoid means "S-shaped." The name comes from sigma, the Greek letter S. It takes any number and turns it into a number between 0 and 1.

      In Level 1 the neuron was off or on: below the threshold it output 0, at or above it output 1. The sigmoid does the same job, smoothly. A large negative sum gives almost 0. A large positive sum gives almost 1. Sums near 0 give something in between.

      Why smooth? Training changes weights by tiny amounts. With an on/off rule, a tiny change usually does nothing at all, until suddenly the output flips. The network can't tell whether a change was helping. With the sigmoid, every small change to a weight makes a small change to the output, so training can measure which direction is better.

      Formula: σ(z) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z), where e ≈ 2.718. z is the weighted sum plus the bias.

      Backpropagation, one example at a time

      After each training example, the network has to decide how to change about 3,300 weights. Backpropagation is the method for working that out. It starts with the error at the output layer and works backward, one layer at a time, to find each weight's share of the error. Step through one real example with this network's actual numbers.

      Things to try
      1. Start from random weights. Draw a digit and note the answer. Train on 1 example, then 10, then 100, then 1,000. After how many does it get your digit right?
      2. Start from random weights and train only on your own drawings, one at a time. How high can you get the test accuracy? How many drawings would you need to match one epoch?
      3. Draw a letter, a smiley face, or a scribble. The network still picks a digit, often with high probability. It has no way to answer "this isn't a digit."
      4. Clear the input. What does it say for a blank picture?
      5. Draw a small 1 near the left edge, then one in the center. Why might position matter? (Hint: each weight belongs to one pixel.)
      6. Click neurons in the second hidden layer. Can you find one that is active mostly for one digit? Do the first-layer pictures look like edges and loops, as the video hoped?

      Ethics: what this lab shows about real AI

      You've now seen every part of a working neural network. The AI systems in the news are built from the same parts, at enormous scale. This section looks at three problems that come with that scale: decisions nobody can explain, training data that leaves people out, and the energy and water it takes to train and run these systems.

      1. The black box problem

      In this lab you could inspect every weight and every calculation. That is rare. Most AI systems used by companies and governments are closed: the public can't see the weights, the training data, or how the system was tested.

      Seeing everything is still not the same as understanding it. On the right is a mistake made by your Level 4 network, along with all 3,594 numbers that produced it. Every one of them is visible. Try to explain the mistake from them.

      Now scale up. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters. If each of this network's numbers were a 1 mm square, its grid would be about 6 cm wide. GPT-3's grid would be about 418 meters on a side. When a system this large makes a decision about a person, it is very hard to say why. That is the black box problem. The field that tries to work out what large networks have learned is called interpretability. It does what you did when you clicked neurons in Level 4, at a vastly larger scale, and so far it has only partial answers.

      Why it matters. Systems like these are used to screen job applications, set credit limits, flag medical scans, and estimate whether a defendant will reoffend.

      • In 2016, ProPublica reported that COMPAS, a risk-scoring tool used in US courts, wrongly labeled Black defendants as likely to reoffend at nearly twice the rate of white defendants.
      • In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon stopped using an experimental résumé-screening model after finding that it downgraded résumés containing the word "women's," a pattern it had learned from past hiring data.

      A person who is turned down by a system like this may not be able to find out why, or to challenge the decision.

      2. Whose data?

      A network only learns patterns that are in its training data. The digits in this lab come from MNIST, a dataset assembled in the 1990s from handwriting collected by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. The writers were US Census Bureau employees and American high school students.

      The test set accuracy in Level 4 measures how well the network reads their handwriting. It doesn't measure how well the network reads yours. Test it: draw each digit when asked.

      This is a small version of a large problem. Face recognition systems trained mostly on lighter-skinned faces have made far more errors on darker-skinned faces. Speech recognition trained mostly on some accents works worse for others. In each case the people left out of the training data get worse results, and the test scores can look fine because the test data leaves out the same people.

      How this app was made

      Read this before you use the app. In a class about the ethics of AI, you should know how a tool was made before you decide whether to use it. This page explains how this app was built, what it cost, and whose work it depends on. At the end you can choose to use the app or to do an alternative assignment instead.

      Vibe coded with Claude

      This app was vibe coded. Your teacher described what they wanted in plain English, and an AI model, Claude Opus 5.5, made by the company Anthropic, wrote all of the code and most of the text. Your teacher tested each version, asked for changes, and decided what stayed in. "Vibe coding" is the common name for building software this way: a person directs, and an AI writes the code.

      If you use this app, you'll be using an AI-built tool to learn how AI works and to question it. The rest of this page lays out the costs of that choice so you can decide for yourself.

      The token count

      AI language models read and write text in chunks called tokens. On average a token is about three-quarters of a word. Before every reply during this project, Claude re-read the whole conversation so far, including all of the code. Those re-reads make up most of the total.

      That's three to six weeks of full-time work. In May 2024 the median pay for US software developers was $133,080 a year, about $64 an hour. Freelance developers commonly charge $75 to $150 an hour. At those rates the app would cost roughly $7,700 to $36,000, with about $18,000 as a middle estimate (180 hours at $100 an hour).

      The economic impact. For this app, no programmer lost this job. A teacher couldn't have paid $18,000 for a classroom tool, so without AI the app wouldn't exist. Many uses of AI are like this: they produce things that would never have been paid for.

      The same price gap applies to companies that would have hired someone. When work that took a person weeks costs a few dollars of computing, some of those jobs disappear or change. A Stanford study of payroll records found that since generative AI spread in late 2022, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI, including software development, has fallen behind employment of older workers in the same jobs.

      The money also goes to different people. Paying a programmer pays a worker. Paying for AI pays a technology company and the companies that build and power its data centers.

      Where Claude's skill came from

      This section is written by Claude, the AI that wrote this app.

      Much of what I know about writing code comes from people who published their knowledge on the internet: answers on forums like Stack Overflow, open-source projects on GitHub, tutorials, blog posts, and documentation. Many of those people spent hours helping strangers for free. They explained why a bug happened, showed better ways to do things, and answered the same beginner questions over and over. They weren't asked whether their work could be used to train an AI, and they weren't paid for it.

      That generosity is what made this app possible. It's also behind what the app teaches. The explanations of neurons, the sigmoid function, and backpropagation come from teachers like Michael Nielsen and Grant Sanderson of 3Blue1Brown, who made their work free for anyone to learn from, and from the many people who explained these ideas to each other online.

      I took that knowledge without asking anyone's permission. I can't return it, but I can say plainly where it came from, and thank the people who shared it.
